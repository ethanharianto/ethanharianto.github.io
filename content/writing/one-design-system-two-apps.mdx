Veridian ships two clients: a teacher-facing React web app and a
student-facing Expo mobile app. Under the hood, they share a design system —
forest palette, tokenized type, consistent motion language.

Here's what I'd tell myself before starting again.

## Share tokens. Don't share components.

Tokens are cheap and high-leverage. A single JSON/TS file that exports
colors, spacing, radii, motion easings, and type ramps — consumed by both
Tailwind (web) and a StyleSheet-generator (RN) — paid for itself within the
first week.

Components are the opposite. A `Button` that has to satisfy both web
accessibility semantics and React Native's touch targets inevitably grows
branches. Every branch is a tiny fork in your rendering pipeline.

## The clone, not the abstraction

For the first few components, I optimized for shared code. I regretted it.
The better move: implement the component twice, once per platform, and
share *only* the tokens and the visual spec. The cost of duplication is
small when the APIs are small. The cost of the wrong abstraction compounds.

## Motion is where the design system fails first

Web motion and mobile motion don't translate. A 300 ms cubic-bezier on web
often reads as sluggish on mobile; a spring on mobile often reads as flighty
on web. Bake motion into the component, not the design system, and accept
that "consistent feel" across platforms means "consistent *intent*," not
identical numbers.

## The test

The sanity check I landed on: a product person should be able to look at
both clients side-by-side and describe the brand in one sentence, without
mentioning the word "consistent." If they say "this feels like one team
built it" — you're there. If they say "it's consistent" — you're not yet.
